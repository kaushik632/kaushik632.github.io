AI in Academic Advising

A faculty-supervised research project at Missouri S&T that extends the ARGObot advising-chatbot methodology with real job-market data, so curriculum recommendations are grounded in what employers actually need — not just what the course catalog lists.

Executive Summary

Built an AI-assisted advising chatbot for the BS Engineering Management program that pairs the full university course catalog (1,990 courses across 54 departments) with a structured job-market corpus of 42 postings across 7 career interest areas. The system maps employer-cited skill themes to specific courses with confidence ratings, surfacing real curriculum gaps that catalog-only advising misses entirely.

01 — The Problem

Catalog-Only Advising Misses What Matters

Academic advising for the BS Engineering Management program traditionally recommends emphasis areas and electives based only on the course catalog — what courses exist — not on what regional employers are actually asking for.

A student choosing between Management of Technology, Industrial Engineering, or a General emphasis has no reliable way to know which option best matches in-demand skills in a field like manufacturing or operations, or where the degree has real skill gaps that need outside coursework or a certification to fill.

Original work in this space (ARGObot) proved that an AI advising chatbot can answer policy questions accurately from a handbook. This project asks a harder question: can the same approach give curriculum advice that's grounded in labor-market evidence, not just document retrieval?

02 — Approach

Extending RAG with Job-Market Grounding

The system extends the ARGObot methodology — retrieval-based and agentic advising chatbot architectures — from a policy-document domain into curriculum advising, pairing academic data with real employer demand.

  • Pairs the full university course catalog with a structured job-market corpus, so advising responses can cite specific employer demand alongside course descriptions.
  • Uses deterministic PDF parsing — not OCR guesswork — to build a verified, machine-readable course catalog directly from the official 475-page university catalog.
  • Maps employer-cited skill themes to specific courses with confidence ratings (Strong / Moderate / Weak / Gap), so the system can name real coverage gaps instead of glossing over them.
  • Evaluated with a RAGAs-style benchmark and sensitivity testing across job-posting subsets, so advising outputs are auditable rather than just plausible-sounding.

04 — Proof of Concept

The Kenji Martin Experiment

To test what the job-market extension actually changes, the project ran a controlled comparison: the same student profile and the same advising question, answered twice — once with only the course catalog available, and once with the catalog plus job-market data.

Student profile: Kenji Martin, a junior in BS Engineering Management who has completed the Foundation and EMGT Core tiers and is choosing an emphasis area, with a stated interest in Manufacturing/Operations.

Question: “Which emphasis area and electives should I choose?”

Dimension Catalog-Only Advising Catalog + Job-Market Advising
Emphasis-area ranking Generic topic-proximity guess Ranked by employer-demand match, citing specific posting frequencies
General-emphasis guidance “Pick whichever discipline appeals to you” Names a specific discipline tied to the two highest-demand skill gaps, with real course-availability counts
Elective recommendation Generic (“well-regarded courses”) A specific course, ranked by how many in-demand skills it covers
Skill gaps with no course Invisible to the system Surfaced explicitly, with a non-course remedy (e.g., a certification)
Claims traceable to evidence No — reasoning is topical Yes — every recommendation cites a specific employer data point

Curriculum data alone can describe what exists. Only labor-market data can say what's actually worth prioritizing, and where the real gaps are. The same student, asking the same question, gets categorically different advice depending on whether job-market grounding is available: specific, ranked, and evidence-backed instead of generic and associative.

05 — Why It Matters

From Generic to Evidence-Backed

The core contribution this project demonstrates is that adding structured labor-market data to an AI advising system fundamentally changes the quality of advice — from generic course-catalog retrieval to specific, ranked, evidence-backed recommendations.

Every claim the system makes is traceable to a specific employer data point. Skill gaps that are invisible to catalog-only systems are surfaced explicitly, along with actionable remedies. Students get advice grounded in what regional employers are actually hiring for, not just what the course catalog happens to list.
